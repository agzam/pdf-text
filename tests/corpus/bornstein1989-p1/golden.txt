Exposure and Affect: Overview and Meta-Analysis

ofResearch, 1968-1987

  Robert F. Bornstein Gettysburg College

  A review and meta-analysis of methodological and subject variables influencing the exposure-affect relationship was performed on studies of the mere exposure effect published in the 20 years following Zajonc's (1968)-seminal monograph. Stimulus type, stimulus complexity, presentation sequence, exposure duration, stimulus recognition, age of subject, delay between exposure and ratings, and maximum number of stimulus presentations all influence the magnitude of the exposure effect. Implications of these findings are discussed in the context of previous reviews of the literature on exposure effects and with respect to prevailing theoretical models of the exposure-affect relationship. Modifications of the 2-factor model of exposure effects that increase the heuristic value of the model are described. A possible evolutionary basis of the exposure effect is discussed.

Psychologists have long observed that repeated, unreinforced exposure results in an increase in positive affect toward a stimulus (Fechner, 1876; Maslow, 1937). A number of early studies in this area (e.g., Meyer, 1903; Mpore & Gilliland, 1924; Washburn, Child, & Abel, 1927) investigated the effects of repeated exposures on affect ratings of music, generally finding more positive ratings with increasing familiarity. Many of these experiments were methodologically unsound, however (i.e., involved improper control conditions or groups or no controls at all), and conclusions based on their results are somewhat tenuous. Additional indirect evidence for the hypothesis that increased exposure leads to more positive affect toward a stimulus came from studies of social interaction (e.g., Festinger, 1951; Homans, 1961;Newcomb, 1963). Although these investigations consistently demonstrated that interaction enhances the attitudes of participants toward each other, it is impossible to tell to what degree this is due to mere exposure, to reinforcement derived from social interaction, or to some combination of these (and other) variables.

With the publication of Zajonc's (1968) monograph on the "Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure," the exposure effect became a topic of investigation in mainstream psychology. Zajonc (1968) defined the exposure effect as the observation that "mere repeated exposure of the individual to a stimulus is a sufficient condition for the enhancement of his attitude toward it. By 'mere exposure' is meant a condition which just makes the given stimulus accessible to the individual's perception" (p. 1). Zajonc thus suggested that simple, unreinforced exposure leads

Empirical and correlational evidence cited by Zajonc (1968) in support of this hypothesis delineated what became the proto typic methodologies used by other researchers to investigate the exposure-affect relationship. The basic design of most laboratory mere exposure research is derived from Zajonc's (1968) Experiments 1-3, and the general approach used by later researchers to examine the exposure-affect relationship in vivo (i.e., in "naturalistic" studies of exposure and affect) is derived from the correlational evidence cited by Zajonc. Thus, the typical laboratory study of exposure and affect involves presenting varying numbers of exposures of neutral visual stimuli (e.g., line drawings, nonsense words), after which some dependent measure of affect regarding each stimulus is collected. Naturalistic studies, which focus on stimuli present in the natural environment (e.g., names of public figures, common items), obtain subjects' affect ratings of these stimuli using procedures similar to those used in laboratory mere exposure studies but with frequency of exposure estimated via some unobtrusive index such as the Thorndike and Lorge (1944) word frequency count or subjects' self-reports. Both laboratory and naturalistic studies of the exposure effect generally utilize within-subjects designs, with each subject providing ratings of a number of stimuli that differ in amount of previous exposure or familiarity.

Zajonc's (1968) initial correlational evidence indicated that relationships exist between the Thorndike and Lorge (1944) word frequency count and the evaluative meaning of a word, as well as between word frequency and attitudes toward the object described by a word. Presumably, these relationships reflect attitude enhancement resulting from repeated exposure to particular words. Because these data are correlational, though, it is impossible to demonstrate any causal relationship between word frequency and evaluative meaning or attitude change. However, Zajonc also presented a series of experiments testing his mere exposure hypothesis. Stronger evidence for the exposure-affect relationship comes from these studies.
